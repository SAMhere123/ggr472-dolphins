Threats Facing Hawaiian Dolphin Species

Hawaiian dolphin species including bottlenose dolphins, pantropical spotted dolphins, spinner dolphins, and rough-toothed dolphins face a range of human-driven threats that jeopardize their long-term viability. Nearshore species such as bottlenose and spinner dolphins are especially vulnerable to entanglement in gillnets, hook-and-line fisheries, and longline gear, with documented cases of drowning, hooking injuries, and gear ingestion (Baird, 2016). Chronic disturbance from tourism, particularly 'swim-with-dolphin' operations, disrupts essential daytime resting behavior in spinner dolphins, which rely on sheltered bays for recovery after nocturnal foraging. These dolphins experience human presence for over 80% of daylight hours, raising concerns about rest deprivation and long-term population impacts (Tyne et al., 2018). Across species, vessel noise, echosounders, and increasing coastal boat traffic can mask communication and echolocation, thus altering behavior and reducing foraging efficiency (Baird, 2016). Additionally, bioaccumulation of persistent organic pollutants (POPs) such as PCBs and pesticides poses immunological and reproductive risks, particularly for fish-eating species like bottlenose and rough-toothed dolphins (Baird, 2016). Together, these pressures, including fishery interactions, disturbance, noise, and pollution, create cumulative, population-level threats for Hawaiian dolphins.
