Threats Facing Hawaiian Dolphins

Hawaiian dolphin species including bottlenose dolphins, spotted dolphins, spinner dolphins, and rough-toothed dolphins face a range of human-driven threats that jeopardize their long-term viability. Nearshore species such as bottlenose and spinner dolphins are especially vulnerable to entanglement in gillnets, hook-and-line fisheries, and longline gear, with documented cases of drowning, hooking injuries, and gear ingestion (Baird, 2016).

Climate Change Impacts on Dolphin Populations

Climate change is also increasingly reshaping the environmental conditions that Hawaiian dolphin populations depend on, altering prey availability, habitat quality, and even the behavior and distribution of dolphins. Rising ocean temperatures and shifting currents affect the location and abundance of prey species, forcing dolphins to adjust their foraging ranges and potentially reducing their energetic efficiency (International Fund for Animal Welfare., 2025). Evidence of long-term climate oscillations such as ENSO events have been shown to influence cetacean presence and distribution in Hawaiian waters, with detection patterns closely tied to climate indices and changing surface-water conditions (Ziegenhorn et al., 2023). These shifts can alter when and where dolphins rest, feed, and travel, all of which are behaviors already tightly constrained in species like spinner dolphins that depend on predictable sheltered bays for daytime rest. Climate-driven stressors also increase disease vulnerability and mortality. Warmer waters and more frequent marine heatwaves have been linked to mass die-offs and weakened immune systems in dolphins globally, with 61% of disease outbreaks occurring during periods of elevated sea surface temperature (Groves, 2023).
Such impacts can reduce local population resilience, making dolphins more sensitive to additional pressures like tourism disturbance. As prey distributions shift and dolphins alter their movement patterns, traditional dolphin-watching hotspots may see changes in sighting frequency, potentially displacing tourism activity or increasing pressure on remaining predictable resting habitats. These ecological changes underscore the importance of adaptive management, such as stricter resting-bay protections and climate informed monitoring, to ensure that dolphin watching practices remain sustainable as environmental conditions continue to change.
