An Overview of the Seven Dolphin Species Found in Hawaiian Waters:

Common Bottlenose Dolphin (Tursiops truncatus)

Common bottlenose dolphins are medium-sized, robust dolphins recognized by their short, well-defined rostrum and curved dorsal fin. In Hawaiian waters, they occur as both a large offshore form and a smaller, island-associated form, with adults typically ranging from 2.5-3.5 m in length (Baird, 2016). Their coloration is gray with a darker cape and lighter belly, and older individuals often accumulate pale linear scars from social interactions and cookie-cutter shark bites. They live in fission-fusion societies, forming fluid groups that change composition throughout the day. Around the Hawaiian Islands, they show strong site fidelity, with genetically distinct populations around Kaua'i/Ni'ihau, O'ahu, Maui Nui, and Hawai'i Island.

Rough-Toothed Dolphin (Steno bredanensis)

Rough-toothed dolphins are slender, long-snouted dolphins with a gently sloping forehead and unusually large flippers (Baird, 2016). Their name comes from the fine vertical ridges on their teeth, though these are rarely visible in the field. They display mottled gray coloration with distinctive blotchy patterns and extensive white scarring on the belly from cookie-cutter shark bites. In Hawai'i, they are frequently seen in deep offshore waters, especially around Kaua'i and Ni'ihau, where they form cohesive groups averaging around 11 individuals. They are agile, surface-active dolphins known for puzzle-solving behavior and for associating with floating debris, fish schools, and occasionally other cetaceans such as false killer whales.

Pantropical Spotted Dolphin (Stenella attenuata)

Pantropical spotted dolphins are small, sleek dolphins widely distributed in warm oceans. Adults develop characteristic spotting that increases with age-dark spots on the belly and light spots on the darker back, though Hawaiian juveniles may appear unspotted (Baird, 2016). They have a slender body, long beak, and tall falcate dorsal fin. These dolphins form large, energetic groups that may number in the hundreds, often engaging in acrobatic leaps and bow-riding. In Hawaiian waters, they are typically found offshore but can overlap with spinner dolphins in deeper nearshore channels. Their social structure is dynamic, and they frequently associate with tuna and seabirds due to shared prey.

Spinner Dolphin (Stenella longirostris)

Spinner dolphins are named for their spectacular spinning leaps, rotating multiple times in the air. They are small, slender dolphins with a long, narrow beak and a distinctive three-tone coloration pattern: dark gray cape, light gray sides, and white belly (Baird, 2016). Hawaiian spinner dolphins follow a strict diel cycle, often feeding offshore at night and resting in sheltered bays during the day (Tyne et al., 2018). Groups are often tightly cohesive during resting periods, exhibiting synchronous diving and reduced acoustic activity. Their acrobatic behavior, large group sizes, and predictable daytime presence in bays make them one of the most recognizable species in Hawai'i.

Striped Dolphin (Stenella coeruleoalba)

Striped dolphins are strikingly patterned, with a bold dark stripe running from the eye to the anal area and another sweeping down the side of the body (Baird, 2016). They are slender, fast-swimming dolphins known for high leaps and energetic behavior. Although more common in temperate and tropical offshore waters worldwide, they are only occasionally recorded in Hawai'i. Adults typically measure 2-2.5 m and display a tri-colored pattern of blue-gray, white, and dark gray. They form large, cohesive groups and often associate with other small delphinids.

Risso's Dolphin (Grampus griseus)

Risso's dolphins are robust, blunt-headed dolphins lacking a distinct beak. They are easily identified by their heavily scarred bodies, as white scratches from social interactions and squid bites accumulate over time, giving adults a pale, almost white appearance (Baird, 2016). They have a tall, curved dorsal fin and a stocky body. They prefer deep offshore waters and feed primarily on squid, using strong suction to capture prey. Groups typically range from 10-50 individuals, though larger aggregations occur. Their calm surfacing behavior contrasts with the more acrobatic Stenella species.

Fraser's Dolphin (Lagenodelphis hosei)

Fraser's dolphins are stocky, muscular dolphins with a short beak and small dorsal fin. They exhibit a distinctive color pattern: a dark band along the flanks, pale belly, and bluish-gray back. Known for their powerful, low-arching surfacing, they often move rapidly in large, tightly packed groups of 100-1000 individuals (Baird, 2016). They prefer deep tropical waters and are infrequently seen near the Hawaiian Islands, though they are present in the broader central Pacific. Their behavior is fast-paced and coordinated, often accompanied by pantropical spotted dolphins or tuna-associated foraging assemblages.
